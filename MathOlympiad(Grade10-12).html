Problem 1

Given that $$\frac{1+3+5+\cdots+(2n-1)}{2+4+6+\cdots+(2n)}=\frac{2011}{2012},$$ determine $n$.

Show Solution
Using the sum formula for arithmetic progressions, $$\frac{1+3+\cdots+(2n-1)}{2+4+\cdots+2n}=\frac{n\cdot\frac{1+(2n-1)}{2}}{n\cdot\frac{2+2n}{2}}=\frac{n}{n+1}=\frac{2011}{2012},$$ so $n=2011$.

Problem 2

Let $ABCD$ be a square and $X$ a point such that $A$ and $X$ are on opposite sides of $CD$. The lines $AX$ and $BX$ intersect $CD$ in $Y$ and $Z$ respectively. If the area of $ABCD$ is $1$ and the area of $XYZ$ is $\tfrac23$, determine the length of $YZ$.

Show Solution
Let $YZ=x$ and let $y$ be the corresponding height of $\triangle XYZ$ from $X$. Since $\triangle XYZ\sim\triangle XAB$ and $AB=1$, the heights $y$ and $1+y$ satisfy $\frac{y}{1+y}=\frac{x}{1}=x$. Hence $\text{area}(XYZ)=\frac{xy}{2}=\frac{y^2}{2(1+y)}=\frac23$, giving $y^2-\frac43y-\frac43=(y+\tfrac23)(y-2)=0$, so $y=2$ and $$x=\frac{y}{1+y}=\frac23.$$

Problem 3

Sixty points on a circle are coloured: thirty red, twenty blue, ten green, dividing the circle into sixty arcs. An arc between a red and green point is assigned $1$; between red and blue, $2$; between blue and green, $3$; between two points of the same colour, $0$. What is the greatest possible sum of all the numbers assigned to the arcs?

Show Solution
Give red score $0$, green score $1$, blue score $2$. The number on an arc is at most the sum of the scores of its endpoints, so the total is at most twice the sum of all sixty scores: $$2(30\cdot0+20\cdot2+10\cdot1)=100.$$ Equality holds when there are no arcs joining two green or two blue points, e.g. by alternating red and non-red points. So the greatest possible sum is $\boxed{100}$.

Problem 4

Let $p$ and $k$ be positive integers, $p$ prime, $k>1$. Prove that there is at most one pair $(x,y)$ of positive integers such that $x^k+px=y^k$.

Show Solution
Case $\gcd(x,p)=1$: then $x$ and $x^{k-1}+p$ are coprime, and since $x(x^{k-1}+p)=y^k$, both factors must be $k$th powers: $x=u^k$, $x^{k-1}+p=v^k$. Then $$p=v^k-u^{k(k-1)}=(v-u^{k-1})\big(v^{k-1}+v^{k-2}u^{k-1}+\cdots+u^{(k-1)^2}\big).$$ Since $p$ is prime and the second factor exceeds $v-u^{k-1}$, we must have $v-u^{k-1}=1$, i.e. $v=u^{k-1}+1$, giving $$p=(u^{k-1}+1)^{k-1}+(u^{k-1}+1)^{k-2}u^{k-1}+\cdots+u^{(k-1)^2},$$ an increasing function of $u$, so at most one $u$ (hence at most one $(x,y)$) works. Case $\gcd(x,p)=p$: writing $x=p^{k-1}u$, $y=pv$ leads to $p^{k(k-2)}u^k+u=v^k$, but one shows $$(p^{k-2}u)^k< p^{k(k-2)}u^k+u < (p^{k-2}u+1)^k,$$ a contradiction. Hence at most one solution exists overall.

Problem 5

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with $AB\ne AC$, orthocentre $H$, circumcentre $O$, and $D$ the midpoint of $BC$. The extensions of $HD$ and $AO$ meet at $P$. Prove that $\triangle AHP$ and $\triangle ABC$ have the same centroid.

Show Solution
Let $AO$ meet the circumcircle again at $X$ (so $AX$ is a diameter). Since $AX$ is a diameter, $\angle ABX=90^\circ$, so $BX\parallel CH$; similarly $CX\parallel BH$. Hence $BHCX$ is a parallelogram, so its diagonal $XH$ passes through $D$, the midpoint of $BC$. Thus $X$ lies on both $AO$ and $HD$, so $X=P$, and $D$ is also the midpoint of $HP$. So $AD$ is a median of both $\triangle ABC$ (median to $BC$) and $\triangle AHP$ (median to $HP$), and since the centroid divides a median in ratio $2:1$ from the vertex, both triangles have their centroid at the same point on $AD$, two-thirds of the way from $A$.

Problem 6

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}$ such that $$f(km)+f(kn)-f(k)f(mn)\ge 1$$ for all $k,m,n\in\mathbb{N}$.

Show Solution
Setting $k=m=n=1$ gives $(f(1)-1)^2\le0$, so $f(1)=1$. Setting $k=1,n=m$ and $k=n,m=1$ gives $$2f(n)-f(n^2)\ge1,\qquad f(n^2)+f(n)-f(n)^2\ge1.$$ Adding: $-f(n)^2+3f(n)\ge2$, i.e. $(f(n)-1)(f(n)-2)\le0$, so $1\le f(n)\le2$ for all $n$. Suppose some $f(n)=a>1$. Using the second inequality and monotonicity of $g(x)=x+\tfrac1x$ on $(1,\infty)$, one shows $f(n^2)\ge b\,f(n)$ where $b=1+\frac{(a-1)^2}{a}>1$. Iterating, $f(n^{2^k})\ge b^k a\to\infty$, contradicting $f\le2$. Hence $f(n)\le 1$ for all $n$, and combined with $f(n)\ge1$, the only solution is $$f(n)\equiv 1.$$

2013 is the first year since the Middle Ages consisting of four consecutive digits. How many such years are there still to come after 2013 (and before the year 10000)?

Show Solution
There are $7$ choices of four consecutive digits ($0,1,2,3$ up to $6,7,8,9$), each giving $4!=24$ arrangements: $7\times24=168$ possibilities. Problem 4

Determine all pairs of polynomials $f,g$ with real coefficients satisfying $x^2\cdot g(x)=f(g(x))$.

Show Solution
Write $f(x)=c_0+c_1x+\cdots+c_nx^n$. Then $x^2g(x)=c_0+c_1g(x)+\cdots+c_ng(x)^n$. The left side and every term but $c_0$ on the right are divisible by $g(x)$, so $c_0=0$ unless $g$ is constant. If $g\equiv K$ constant: $Kx^2=f(K)$ forces $K=0$, $f$ any polynomial with $f(0)=0$. If $g$ non-constant, $c_0=0$: dividing by $g(x)$, $x^2=c_1+c_2g(x)+\cdots+c_ng(x)^{n-1}$. With $\deg g=m$, the right side has degree $m(n-1)=2$, giving $(m,n)=(1,3)$ or $(2,2)$:
  • $g(x)=ax+b$, $f(x)=\dfrac{x(x-b)^2}{a^2}$ (any $a\ne0,b$);
  • $g(x)=\dfrac{x^2-c_1}{c_2}$, $f(x)=c_1x+c_2x^2$ ($c_2\ne0$, $c_1$ arbitrary).
These, together with the $g\equiv0$ family, are all solutions.

Problem 5

Coins are placed on a $20\times13$ board. Two coins are neighbours if they share a row or column with no coin between them. Find the largest number of coins so that no coin has more than two neighbours.

Show Solution
On an $m\times n$ board ($m,n>1$) the maximum is $m+n$: achieved by placing coins along the top row, the rightmost column, and the bottom-left square. By induction on $m+n$ (using the pigeonhole principle: a row with $\ge3$ coins forces a "lonely" column that can be deleted), one shows $m+n$ cannot be exceeded. For $20\times 13$: $20+13=\boxed{33}$.

Problem 1

Determine the last two digits of the product of the squares of all positive odd integers less than $2014$.

Show Solution
The product $P$ of all odd integers less than $2014$ includes $25$ as a factor and is odd, so its last two digits are $25$ or $75$; write $P=100n+25$ or $100n+75$. Squaring either form gives last two digits $\boxed{25}$ (in fact the last three digits are $625$).

Problem 2

Given $\dfrac{a-b}{c-d}=2$ and $\dfrac{a-c}{b-d}=3$, determine $\dfrac{a-d}{b-c}$.

Show Solution
Let $x=c-d$, $y=b-d$. Then $a-d=(c-d)+(a-c)=x+3y$ and $a-d=(b-d)+(a-b)=y+2x$, so $x+3y=y+2x\Rightarrow x=2y$. Then $a-d=5y$ and $b-c=(b-d)-(c-d)=y-x=-y$, so $$\frac{a-d}{b-c}=\boxed{-5}.$$

Problem 5

Let $n>1$. An $n\times n$ grid has $n$ green and $n$ blue unit squares (rest white). Are there more colourings with no two green in a row and no two blue in a column, or colourings with no two green and no two blue in the same row?

Show Solution
If $n$ green squares occupy distinct rows, each row has $n-1$ remaining squares, giving $(n-1)^n$ ways to place $n$ non-repeating-row blue squares. For the other count, if $x_i$ is the number of non-green squares in column $i$ ($\sum x_i=n^2-n$), the number of column-safe blue placements is $x_1x_2\cdots x_n\le\left(\frac{n^2-n}{n}\right)^n=(n-1)^n$ by AM–GM, with strict inequality possible. So there are (weakly) more colourings of the first type (no two green, no two blue in the same row) than of the second type.

Problem 2

Determine all pairs of real numbers $a,x$ satisfying $5x^3+ax^2+8=0$ and $5x^3+8x^2+a=0$.

Show Solution

Problem 3

A divisor $d$ of $n$ is special if $d+1$ is also a divisor. Prove at most half the divisors of $n$ are special, and find all $n$ for which exactly half are special.

Show Solution
If $d\ge\sqrt n$ is special then $n/d\ge n/(d+1)+1$, i.e. $n\ge d(d+1)>d^2\ge n$, a contradiction. So only divisors $<\sqrt n$ can be special; since divisors pair up as $(a,n/a)$ across $\sqrt n$, at most half can be special. For exactly half: $n$ is not a perfect square, and every divisor below $\sqrt n$ must be special, forcing $1,2,\dots,k$ (the largest such $k<\sqrt n$) all to be divisors, with $n=k(k+1)$. One checks $k-1\mid 2$, giving $k\in\{1,2,3\}$, so $$n\in\{2,\,6,\,12\}.$$

Problem 6

Suppose $a$ is an integer and $n!+a$ divides $(2n)!$ for infinitely many positive integers $n$. Prove $a=0$.

Show Solution
Note $(2n)!=\binom{2n}{n}(n!)^2\equiv\binom{2n}{n}a^2 \pmod{n!+a}$, so $n!+a$ divides $a^2\binom{2n}{n}$. Using $\binom{2n}{n}\le4^n$, one shows for $a\ne 0$ and $n$ large that $$\frac{a^2\binom{2n}{n}}{n!+a}<1,$$ (comparing the factorial growth of $n!$ against the exponential $4^n$), which forces $a^2\binom{2n}{n}=0$ — impossible unless it isn't actually divisible, contradicting the hypothesis for infinitely many $n$. Hence $a=0$ (and indeed $n!\mid(2n)!$ always).

Problem 4

Let $A$ be a set of reals with (a) $\sqrt{n^2+1}\in A$ for all positive integers $n$; (b) $x,y\in A\Rightarrow x-y\in A$. Prove every integer is a product of two different elements of $A$.

Show Solution
Property (b) gives $0\in A$, $x\in A\Rightarrow-x\in A$, $x,y\in A\Rightarrow x+y\in A$, and $kx\in A$ for all integers $k$ (once $x\in A$). Choosing $k,l,m,n$ with $1=k^2(m^2+1)-l^2(n^2+1)$ (e.g. $k=3,m=1,l=1,n=4$), we get $$1=\left(3\sqrt2-\sqrt{17}\right)\left(3\sqrt2+\sqrt{17}\right)$$ with both factors in $A$ and distinct. Then for any integer $p$, $$p=\left(3p\sqrt2-p\sqrt{17}\right)\left(3\sqrt2+\sqrt{17}\right),$$ a product of two elements of $A$ that are distinct (since equality would force $p$ irrational). This proves the claim.

Problem 5

SAMF operates a tree of flights rooted at Kimberley (every airport reachable from Kimberley in exactly one way). Let $t$ be the number of terminal (degree-1, non-root) airports. SAMF closes $k$ airports, keeping the rest connected to Kimberley; let $C$ be the number of ways. Prove $$\binom{t}{k}\le C\le\binom{t+k-1}{k}.$$

Show Solution
Model the network as a tree rooted at Kimberley. Closing airports corresponds to removing a vertex together with its entire "downstream" subtree. Removing any $k$ of the $t$ leaves is always valid, giving $C\ge\binom{t}{k}$. For the upper bound, for each leaf $T_i$ let $S_i$ be the nearest ancestor of degree $>2$ (or the root). Removing valid sets of $k$ vertices corresponds to choosing, for each leaf-branch, how many of the bottom vertices (up to the branch point) to remove; the number of ways to distribute $k$ removals among $t$ branches is at most the number of solutions to $\sum k_i=k$ with $0\le k_i\le k$, namely $\binom{t+k-1}{k}$ (with equality when every branch has at least $k$ removable vertices, and a strict inequality otherwise due to increased dependency between branches). Hence $$\binom{t}{k}\le C\le\binom{t+k-1}{k}.$$
